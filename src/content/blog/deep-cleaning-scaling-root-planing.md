If your dentist has recommended a deep cleaning, you might be wondering what that means and whether it's really necessary. The good news is that this common procedure is nothing to worry about—it's simply a more thorough cleaning designed to help your gums heal and protect your oral health for years to come.

## Understanding the Difference Between Regular and Deep Cleanings

A standard dental cleaning focuses on removing plaque and tartar from the surfaces of your teeth, primarily above and just below the gumline. This preventive care works well for patients with healthy gums.

However, when **gum disease** (also called periodontal disease) develops, harmful bacteria begin to accumulate in deeper pockets that form between your teeth and gums. A regular cleaning can't reach these areas effectively. That's where **scaling and root planing**—commonly called a deep cleaning—comes in.

During this procedure, your dental team carefully removes plaque and tartar buildup from **below the gumline** and smooths the root surfaces of your teeth. This "planing" process helps your gums reattach more firmly to your teeth, reducing pocket depth and giving harmful bacteria fewer places to hide.

## Signs You Might Need a Deep Cleaning

Your dentist may recommend scaling and root planing if they observe:

- **Gum pockets measuring 4 millimeters or deeper** (healthy pockets are typically 1-3 mm)
- Bleeding gums during brushing or flossing
- Persistent bad breath that doesn't improve with regular oral care
- Red, swollen, or tender gums
- Visible tartar buildup below the gumline
- Early signs of bone loss on dental X-rays

Many patients throughout Bergen County and beyond are surprised to learn they have gum disease because the early stages are often painless. Regular dental checkups are essential for catching these problems before they progress.

## What to Expect During and After the Procedure

**During your appointment**, your dental team will likely numb the treatment area to ensure your comfort. Depending on the extent of cleaning needed, the procedure may be completed in one visit or split into multiple appointments, treating different sections of your mouth each time.

You may experience some mild sensitivity or tenderness for a few days afterward—this is completely normal and typically resolves quickly. Your gums might also feel slightly swollen or bleed a bit during brushing, but this should improve as healing progresses.

**After your deep cleaning**, your dentist will likely recommend:

- Using a soft-bristled toothbrush and brushing gently
- Rinsing with warm salt water to soothe your gums
- Avoiding very hot, cold, or crunchy foods for a day or two
- Taking over-the-counter pain relievers if needed

**Here's a practical tip you can use today:** If you notice your gums bleeding when you brush or floss, don't avoid those areas—gentle, consistent cleaning actually helps reduce inflammation over time. Many people mistakenly think bleeding means they should brush less, but the opposite is usually true.

## The Importance of Follow-Up Care

A deep cleaning is often just the first step in treating gum disease. Your dental team will likely schedule **periodontal maintenance visits** every three to four months to monitor your gum health and prevent bacteria from building up again. These follow-up appointments are crucial for maintaining the progress you've made.

With proper home care and regular professional cleanings, many patients see significant improvement in their gum health. Pockets can shrink, bleeding can stop, and you can avoid more serious complications like tooth loss down the road.
